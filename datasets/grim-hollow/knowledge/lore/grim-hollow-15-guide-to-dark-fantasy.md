# Chapter 15: Guide to Dark Fantasy

What makes a dark fantasy campaign different from a typical 5e campaign? What does a Game Master need to do to bring a horror-flavored tinge to the encounters and adventures? How can the players—and the characters they bring into the story—add to the style of dark fantasy?

This chapter provides insights and tips into the genres and tropes that come together to make dark fantasy a reality within your games. From long-term goals to short-term tweaks, read on to gain more insight into bringing the potential of a Grim Hollow campaign to life.

## Dark Fantasy Styles

Dark fantasy is a genre of story and game play that encompasses a number of different styles and subgenres. Each style is discussed here in greater detail. You may choose to mix elements from multiple styles in constructing your adventures or focus exclusively on a single style that is most enjoyable for your group.

Typical dark fantasy is like film noir, in the sense that both genres rely on one foundational truth: there are no truly benevolent heroes left—and those who are don’t stay alive long enough to become famous. In both genres, there is a constant tension between doing the right thing for altruistic reasons and doing the smart thing that brings you closer to your goals and helps you survive.

In dark fantasy, characters and NPCs alike struggle with impossible moral and ethical choices. Even supposedly good people are flawed, capable of tragic and unspeakable deeds. Meanwhile, villains don’t think twice about committing atrocities. A happy ending where everyone survives intact is unlikely.

Dark fantasy adopts realism, reflecting the world as we fear it to be rather than the one we hope for. The horror and ills of a real society exist in a dark fantasy world—all of the moral failings of people and the societies they create are on display, and fighting the status quo generally leads to unhappy and unfortunate consequences for the defiant parties.

When it comes to war, a dark fantasy setting is critical of any notion of glory and heroism in battle. Emphasized instead are sacrifices made to “win,” the costs to innocent lives, the destruction of homes, the toll on the soldiers, and the horror witnessed by combatants on both sides of the conflict. And, of course, the individuals who seek (or seek to maintain) power as a result of the fighting rarely pay any price at all. War is a violent and devastating business. Yet human failings such as greed, jealousy, ambition, hatred, zealotry, and even the destructive aspects of love make conflict inevitable.

As for magic in a dark fantasy setting, its use often comes at an unspeakable cost and with terrible consequences when things go wrong. Infernal pacts cost souls and unleash eldritch horrors. Arcane studies take a toll on the body and the mind of the practitioners. The slightest miscalculation when channeling the power of magic can lead to dire consequences for the casters and the innocent people around them.

That said, hope glimmers into a dark fantasy world like a fragment of light through a narrow curtain. Plagued by malice and hardship, it’s important that everything happens as a consequence of choices the characters made. A world that’s cruel for cruelty’s sake is not only illogical, but it’s also boring and hard to navigate for players. Using dark fantasy themes purely for shock value eventually wears down your players, and likely yourself as well.

Dark fantasy serves a purpose—foregrounding the dangers of the campaign, engaging the players with realism, and allowing them to feel the impact of their choices, for good or ill. Hope is what makes your dark fantasy world meaningful.

### Grimdark

Grimdark is an even more nihilistic subgenre of dark fantasy. Grimdark worlds aim less to mimic the realities of a corrupt society and prefer to magnify the depths of human desperation or depravity. In a grimdark world, society has already collapsed. There are no glimmers of hope and no possibility that evil will ever be punished. Indeed, evil deeds may be the only means for survival.

Mortal lives are cheap and frequently meaningless in grimdark settings. Enforced suffering, such as the sacrifice of a thousand innocent souls to sate an eldritch god, may be a necessary toll to keep the light of humanity alive. Resources are scarce and conflict is ceaseless. Rather than bastions of light in a perilous world, nowhere is truly safe. Death is inevitable and ever prowling, whether it comes in the guise of a maniacal priest, a flesh-eating affliction, or a terrifying, otherworldly monster.

Despite how dire grimdark worlds can be, the fun of such settings is in the excessively dystopian worldbuilding and the challenge of surviving such hostile circumstances. Your characters still have motivations and goals. But the question your adventures will explore is not whether you can succeed, but rather how long your characters can last?

### Horror

Horror is by no means the easiest genre to create stories for. It relies on the right amount of tension, atmosphere, and imagery to work. If these are lacking, or if they’re overdone, it can quickly turn into unintended comedy.

There are many nuances of horror, including terror, dread, and revulsion. Revulsion is easy to elicit; it can come from something as straightforwardly disgusting as slipping on a pile of corpses and falling into a pool of blood and viscera.

Dread is more difficult. It requires slow-building tension and a description of an atmosphere that is both immediate and mysterious. If you can give the players the feeling that something terrible is about to happen, but they don’t quite know when it will happen, you have initiated a bout of potential dread. The longer you can sustain this buildup, the more powerful the release when the dreadful event happens.

You can describe the characters going down a dark corridor or a shadowy forest, where they catch glimpses of creatures moving from the corner of their eyes. They know they are being followed and an attack is imminent—but they don’t know when or by what. False releases of the dread give temporary relief but ultimately heighten the buildup.

The final layer is terror, a release of emotion that comes from shock and the realization that something horrible is happening that is beyond the players’ control. This is difficult to evoke since most players often feel their characters are in control of situations—thanks to their power and abilities. The key is in catching them unawares and upending their expectations.

For example, the party could defeat the seemingly corrupt inquisitor, only for his head to split open and reveal a monstrous, tentacled thing using human flesh as its shell. From a moment like this you not only get revulsion but a possible heart-pounding kick in the gut.

Horror pairs well into dark fantasy because it helps portray a world where frightening and dangerous things exist. NPCs are useful for building this atmosphere. A traumatized villager sitting in a tavern might fearfully describe their encounter with a monster from the woods, even thinking of it causing their hands to shake.

### Dark Fairy Tale

A dark, overgrown forest where wolves and witches roam. An inn owned by an outwardly jolly old fellow who kills and robs weary travelers as they sleep. A cursed noble who must slay those they marry on their wedding night. Evil fairies whose gifts are never as they seem, helping and hindering those who accept them. These are some of the many tropes of dark fairy tales.

The dark fairy tale is another dark fantasy subgenre which leans toward fantasy instead of realism. Situations begin as outwardly normal, even hopeful. Then the characters discover that the reality of their situation is much more bizarre than originally understood, and far beyond their control.

The most important aspect of creating a dark fairy tale setting is an awareness of the elements used in such stories. Reading original stories of the Brothers Grimm will give you an idea, as well as more modern takes by fantasy authors. In these tales, the innocent are really monsters, the monsters often misunderstood, and the evil brought into the world can come from any source.

### Sword and Sorcery

Exemplified in the works of authors Robert E. Howard and Michael Moorcock, the sword and sorcery genre sets aside idealism for a world soaked in mud, blood, and magic. Like the dark fantasy genre, sword and sorcery approaches a fantastic setting from an amoral perspective, with a particular focus on action. Characters live in a gritty, violent world of barbarism, corrupt kings, evil sorcerers, sword-wielding warriors, and horrifying beasts that threaten innocent folks. It can be set in a fallen kingdom or a post-apocalyptic desert wasteland. Most of all, the combat scenes are visceral, dynamic, and absolutely blood-soaked.

The other half of the equation, magic, is similarly important. Magic is essentially dangerous, difficult to control, and forbidden. Those who use it pay a high price in terms of blood, souls, or their own humanity.

## Conflict and Resolution

Good encounters and adventures revolve around conflict, the engine that drives all stories. If characters must summon all their skills, wits, and resources to resolve a conflict, they are likely to be challenged and fulfilled. As such, the quality of the conflict determines the quality of the narrative.

When designing conflicts for narrative, pay attention to three critical questions:

What? Specifically, what is the conflict about? Who is involved, what is at stake, and what might be necessary to resolve the conflict?

So What? The most violent and dramatic conflicts fail to resonate if the consequences never touch the characters, the things that the characters care about, or the setting as a whole. The most impactful conflicts touch the characters and everything they care about in profound ways.

Now What? What do the characters need to do to resolve the conflict? How does the conflict and its resolution interact with the characters’ immediate and long-term goals?

## Conflict Scale

For games set in a large and complex world such as Etharis, it’s best to focus on three kinds and scales of conflict: character-based, party-based, and world-based.

### Character

In most campaigns that draw upon the characters’ stories, each player develops their character’s history or background early in the process of campaign planning and character creation. Before the players do this, it helps to explain the kind of world Etharis is to your players, allowing them to make informed decisions that fit into the setting.

When players establish their characters in Etharis, they should think about and communicate their characters’ goals, desires, flaws, bonds, and personalities. In essence, they are bringing with them their own conflicts, which can form the seeds of a story. Take notes as they relate their backstories and character concepts, and listen for salient points that you can use for the plots and conflicts of your campaign. Not only do your efforts work to enrich your tale, you can draw your players deeper into the story, as they feel they have a stake in the game.

A character story contains elements the GM can use to motivate them on all sorts of adventures. The character should have a long-term goal (marry into the local merchant’s family) and short-term goals (catch the thief of their family’s stolen signet ring). If you offer them a way to get closer to these goals, the character probably takes it. Put an obstacle between them and these goals, and they’ll try to get around it. (Conversely, if you offer them an adventure that doesn’t get them any closer to those goals, they might ignore it.)

The rival suitor for the hand of the merchant’s scion in marriage may make a good villain or foil in the initial adventures in the campaign. The thief of the signet ring could be tied to a larger threat that reveals itself later in the campaign as well.

#### Moral Complexity

With dark fantasy and the Grim Hollow character options that support play, you might have characters that are seemingly shady, evil, or twisted before the campaign even starts. Or these characters might be kind-hearted folk who have potentially sinister goals. Dark fantasy is a genre that accepts characters can have noble reasons for doing evil deeds, and evil characters often have a relatable cause to their malice.

Enhance the character conflict of your campaign by offering flawed characters paths of light and goodness toward their otherwise evil goals. Helping the people of the town overcome a supernatural threat might be the fastest way to achieving revenge.

Conversely, characters that seem good and strive for positive goals should be challenged by offering them dark shortcuts or impossible decisions. The character that wants to become a paragon of light might have to do questionable things on that journey. It’s in this juxtaposition of light and dark, good and evil, or bravery and cowardice that makes for the most engaging stories.

### Party

Party-level conflicts are not when characters are in conflict with each other. Rather, they are conflicts important to the group that the characters must work together to resolve. A story hook or some other impetus leads them to the conflict, or the conflict comes to them directly.

If you find yourself stuck for ideas, this campaign guide contains plenty of cults, secret organizations, monstrous abominations, maniacal wizards, and eldritch entities to put in front of your characters. A string of antagonists leading to a primary foe can keep your party engaged for an entire campaign if presented correctly.

Remember to focus on what the characters are trying to achieve when imagining these conflicts. It’s not enough for the party to learn about the sorcerer Gorm while they are saving the noble—they might decide it’s not their problem, as long as the noble is safe. To ensure the heroes care about this new conflict, it needs to get in the way of the goal they are already pursuing.


### World

World conflicts occur as the backdrop to your party’s adventures and character- or party-based conflicts. The scale of these conflicts might be local, regional, or global. They include such things as the decimation of a village by the minions of the Great Beast, the war between nations like Ostoya and the Bürach Empire, a shadow war between various factions like the Arcanist Inquisition and an arcane college, ongoing disasters such as the Weeping Pox, or any trouble that is larger than a single string of adventures can address.

These conflicts are meant to ground the party’s adventures in a living, breathing world filled with power shifts and constant change, where things happen that are much bigger than they are. The characters may influence the outcome of these conflicts or vice versa, should their story take them there.

Remember that tying a story into a world conflict adds texture to the story but doesn’t necessarily give the characters more reason to get involved. Over time, you may find ways to organically weave character, party, and world conflicts together into a cohesive plotline. You don’t necessarily have to do it overnight; take your time to plan how the story may unfold. See how a character’s backstory may connect to the party’s quest, and how that party conflict may link to a conflict currently brewing in the region.

Having said that, don’t be married to your plans. Roleplaying is shared storytelling, and your players may have their own ideas that may ultimately prove more satisfying. Be open to these ideas as they may organically enrich your conflict, and therefore your narrative.

## Progression

Just as the pacing of your sessions is a strong contributor to the flow of your campaign, so is the rate and the method by which your players level up. While the conventional method of level via Experience Points is a valid approach, it does have shortcomings.

In particular, it focuses mostly on killing enemies through combat encounters. If combat-based progression works well within your campaign, then this method is fine: your campaign can continue using what works well. However, if you want a more narrative-based game, encourage players to roleplay more, or simply want to try something new, consider one of the options below.

### Milestone Progression

To follow the milestone progression process, at the end of a session simply ask the following question: “Has this session been a defining moment for the current story arc of our campaign?”

If the answer is yes, and you feel it makes sense to do so, award the party with a level increase. There is no perfect pacing set in stone when it comes to awarding milestones, and providing advice is difficult as every group is unique.

For some campaign types and players, leveling after ever session works. Sometimes this leads to the power curve rising too steeply in relation to the progress of the plot.

For some campaigns it’s often best to do it about every 2-3 sessions. This is especially true if your group plays more frequently. However, growing in power too quickly can undermine the sense of danger in your dark fantasy setting, and so levelling up every 3-5 sessions may feel more appropriate. The important aspect is making the tone of the campaign match the rate at which characters grow in power.

Milestone progression focuses on the achievements of your party in a reactive manner. While it is possible to plan milestones ahead of time, the best results come from identifying and awarding them upon completion, or slightly before. After all, it is much easier to identify meaningful milestones after (or just before) they happen. If you have pre-planned milestones, you might be tempted to dictate the party’s behavior, which your players will likely resent.

### Goal-Based Progression

The Hunt Begins

This is a proactive approach where the players direct the pace of the game by choosing their own milestones in the form of goals. While this can be more difficult to pull off, it can increase player buy-in and engagement. It can make planning in advance more difficult, you can mitigate this by asking players to choose the next session’s goals at the end of each session. That way you have time to reflect on the goals as you set up the next session.

To run a campaign with goal-based progression, there are two important steps to take. First you should establish the world and ensure your players know enough about what’s going on to set interesting goals for their characters. This is best achieved by running a short, structured story arc at the beginning of the campaign introducing major themes and influencing factors. Step two is to openly communicate with the party and ask, “What do you want to do?” offering suggestions when the party is unsure. This gets the party involved in shaping the story. Once the party has completed a goal milestone, allocate experience or give them a level depending on the difficulty of the goal.

## Encounter Difficulty

Establishing the proper level of difficulty in your encounters is as much an art as it is a science. Set the encounter difficulty too low, and you’ll hear complaints about the adventure being too easy. Set it too high, and you’ll end up killing the party before you can conclude the story. Finding the right balance is key to an enjoyable and rewarding experience.

Before deciding on an appropriate level for combat encounters, it helps to inform players about what to expect in terms of difficulty. Without giving details away, let them know if you plan on introducing challenging encounters that could end in defeat. That way, your players aren’t caught completely off guard when the difficulty scales up. When scaling combat encounters, you have several options to choose from.

### Alter the Resting Rules

Rests are crucial for managing encounter, adventure, and campaign difficulty. Characters are more powerful and capable within an encounter when they have access to their full resources, especially spell slots and abilities that require Long Rests to recover.

For creating a more challenging and tense campaign, the next chapter provides alternative resting rules. If you use those rules, see how they change the dynamics and difficulty of your encounters and adventures before using the rest of the suggestions in this section. Moving to the new resting system might be more than enough to provide a heightened challenge to your players.

### Increase Monster Challenges

A standard way for raising the difficulty level of encounters is to simply use more monsters or use stronger monsters.

More Hit Points. The first and least intrusive step is to use the standard encounter building rules for fifth edition, but increase the number of Hit Points that creatures have. Rather than using the average Hit Points based on a creature’s Hit Point Dice, use 75% of their maximum potential Hit Points. To make monsters that last longer but not hit harder, give monsters maximum potential Hit Points.

For example, a monster with 10d8 Hit Point Dice and a 16 Constitution would have an average Hit Point maximum of 75: 45 Hit Points from their Hit Point Dice and 30 Hit Points for a +3 Constitution modifier per Hit Point Die.

If you used the maximum Hit Points possible, this would instead by 110 Hit Points instead of 75, allowing the monster to potentially survive a couple of extra attacks from the characters and perhaps do more damage themselves.

More Monsters. Adding one or more additional monsters to a combat also increases the challenge—usually more significantly than simply increasing a monster’s Hit Points. This provides an extra threat to the characters with its own set of actions.

If using this method, start with the lowest Challenge Rating (CR) monster first, adding more fodder for the characters that are easier to kill but also potentially distracting the characters from the greater threat.

Stronger Monsters. The final means of making the threat greater within the realms of the opposing creatures is to use higher CR monsters than you normally would. This is often more challenging to do as a game master, for you need to find a monster that still fits the adventure and the story.

### Create Situational Challenges

Another way to scale the difficulty is to design the battlefield in such a way that it hinders the party or helps the monsters. This can be highly effective—and sometimes too effective if not done carefully. Use this method sparingly, as if it is used too often and outside the bounds of the logical flow of the story and the setting, it begins to look overly punitive and even unfair.

#### Difficult or Dangerous Terrain

For example, the terrain of an encounter may include a substance that damages anyone on the ground. If the characters have no access to flight or other means of moving without touching the ground, they are damaged by the terrain. If all the monsters can fly or otherwise avoid the terrain without being hindered, this makes the encounter much more difficult that on an even playing field. Doing this once likely provides an interesting challenge. Doing this several encounters in a row seems punitive.

Lesser challenges, such as swamps with knee-high water, slippery glacial floors and walls, areas with high winds that make flying difficult, slime-covered surfaces that discourage running, rooms filled with poison gas, cold places that sap one’s strength—are other ways to turn the terrain against the party.

Mechanically, these may cause effects such as making players move at half speed, forcing them to make saving throws against condition, preventing them from flying, and so on. More interesting are areas like this that the characters can find a way to use against the monsters with some effort or witty planning.

#### Advantageous Terrain

Intelligent monsters know to pick terrain that works with their skills and abilities. Thus, incorporeal undead like ghosts and wraiths can ambush characters in narrow corridors, where they can fade through the walls and floors to escape retaliation. Goblin Warriors can used ranged attacks through arrow slits, gaining Three-Quarters Cover from the characters. Abominations can engage on slime-covered floors that are difficult terrain for everyone but themselves. Earth elementals can fight in pitch-black rooms where their Tremorsense is extremely effective.

You have the license to be creative in how you want to use the terrain to give your monsters the edge in battle. It makes them seem more of a threat to your players, even if it is only for a short time.

### Introduce Monsters in Waves

Another way to subtly scale the difficulty is to have the monsters appear and attack in waves. These waves may come in at the top of each round or during a lull in combat. The first wave would function simply as cannon fodder, lulling the players into a false sense of security. Players who tend to use their most powerful spells and abilities early in a combat will easily defeat these weaker monsters.

Subsequent waves would hold deadlier monsters who arrive after the characters have likely unloaded with their most powerful attacks. This means the subsequent waves have a chance to survive for a few more turns than they would otherwise.

Thus, for the first wave, the characters may face a few weaker guards on duty in the castle’s main hall. Later in the same combat, a squad of elite guards enters the fray, having heard the commotion. Subsequent to the elite guards engaging the character, a pair of heavily armored captains show up with their pet troll as the last line of defense.

This tactic has the effect of tempting players to exhaust their resources in early waves. Even against experienced players, you may mix in this technique to cause them to hold back their abilities early on, in case a more formidable foe could be on its way.

Finally, this technique gives you leeway to invisibly scale down your encounter when needed. If your players are having a difficult time with the first or second wave, you can reduce the monsters in the third wave, or do without further waves altogether.
